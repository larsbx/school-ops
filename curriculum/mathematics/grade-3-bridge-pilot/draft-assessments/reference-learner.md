# Grade 3 arithmetic bridge — learner reference form (DRAFT, no answers)

**This is a fixed, publicly exposed example**, not a fresh independent mastery test. Proposed 20–25 minutes, **not an approved timed limit**. Give equivalent large-print, braille, oral, scribed, tactile-tile or drawing response modes. Adult may read a prompt and provide empty grids/counters, **not complete the representation or reason**. State the unit or whole in each response. No source text or personal learner data is used.

The three sections check **separate** local planning targets: `G3M-P1` thousands and comparisons, `G3M-P2` split arrays, `G3M-P3` equally sized parts of the **same whole**. Work on scrap paper or with movable squares; explain with words or demonstration.

## A — Four-place units and comparisons (G3M-P1)

**S1.** Build or describe **5,006** with thousands, hundreds, tens and ones. Give its expanded sum and explain both zeros. An empty place is still a place in the notation.

**S2.** Compare **2,480** and **2,804** using `<`, `>` or `=`. Name the *first place from the left* that differs and state what the digits there mean.

**S3.** A classmate claims, “**3,099 > 3,909** because 99 is larger than 9.” Is that true? Correct or defend the claim with a place-value explanation that does not just repeat the numerical answer.

## B — Rows, columns and disjoint splits (G3M-P2)

**S4.** Build a rectangle of **4 rows with 8 unit squares per row**. Draw a vertical cut after column 3. Give the left and right products and show why their sum equals the entire rectangle. Label rows and columns.

**S5.** Draw or describe **6 rows of 3 unit squares**. Split the columns into **1 and 2**. State the two subarray totals and the original total, without counting a square twice.

**S6.** Someone writes `4 × (2 + 3) = 8 + 3 = 11`. Is the total right? Repair the expression using a row-and-column explanation and name the mistake.

## C — Equal regions of one unchanged whole (G3M-P3)

For each task, *equal* means parts of equal **area/number of identically sized squares**, not merely any groups whose totals add up. Use different copies of the specified whole if showing both halves and fourths.

**S7.** A whole rectangle contains **12 same-sized squares**. Show a half of it and a fourth of it using **two copies of that same-sized whole**. How many squares belong to each share? Explain why the halves and fourths are equal parts in their respective copies.

**S8.** A **4 × 4** grid is one whole. Mark its **four equal-area parts** with boundaries. How many small squares does each fourth contain? Explain why four pieces of different sizes would not be four fourths.

**S9.** You have **two identical eight-square rectangles**. Shade **four** small squares in the first as one equal half and **two** in the second as one equal fourth. Which share is larger *for these identical wholes*, and what makes the comparison legitimate? Explain without relying only on the symbols.

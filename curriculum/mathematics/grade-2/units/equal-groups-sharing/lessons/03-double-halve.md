# Episode 3 — Doubling and halving as inverses

- Lesson ID: **L-MATH-001-03-03**; **status: draft**; arithmetic / O-MATH-001-013 (double, halve and equal sharing).
- Prerequisite: E1–E3 with individual review; sharing into two equal groups observed or modeled and rechecked.
- Materials: counters, two equal labeled boxes, pencil; proposed 25 minutes, adjustable.

## Trivium actions

**Grammar:** *double* means two equal copies of a quantity; *half* is one of two equal parts of a whole. A half here is a whole-number count only when the objects can be shared evenly between two containers.

**Logic:** doubling and halving undo each other for the equal two-group cases in this route. Test by reconstructing the original total; do not claim that an odd collection can be split into two equal whole-number groups.

**Rhetoric:** explain how the two copied/halved collections preserve the total; state when a leftover blocks an equal whole-object share.

## Teaching and independently checkable work

1. **Model (6 min):** Copy 6 counters into two boxes: 6 + 6 = 12. Share the 12 back into two boxes: each has 6. Explain the inverse with the same units.
2. **Guided (7 min):** Double 9. Then halve the result. **Key:** 9 + 9 = 18, then 18 = 9 + 9, half is 9.
3. **Counterexample (4 min):** Put 13 whole counters into two boxes as evenly as possible. **Key:** six per box with **one left over**, so no equal split into two whole-number groups. This is not the claim that mathematical halves of 13 do not exist.
4. **Independent practice/exit (8 min):** Use [P7–P8](../practice-learner.md), then ask an *unseen* 10 → 20 → 10 counter-model check. **Key:** P7 double 8 = 16; P8 half of 18 = 9; independent extension: 10 + 10 = 20 and each half is 10. Require both an accurate model and an explanation of why the reverse operation works.

Use objects and access supports as needed. Do not infer timed fluency or general fraction competence.
